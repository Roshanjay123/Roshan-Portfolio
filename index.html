PhD Research & Digital Construction

Concrete 3D Printing Construction

Experimental and numerical investigation of fresh-state behaviour, extrudability, buildability and structural stability of 3D printed concrete for automated construction.

PhD Research

From Material Extrusion to Structural Stability

My PhD research investigated one of the central challenges in concrete 3D printing: developing a material that can be continuously extruded while gaining sufficient early-age strength and stiffness to support successive printed layers.

The work combined experimental testing with numerical modelling to understand material flow, layer stability, deformation and structural failure during additive manufacturing.

Research areas

  • Fresh-state rheology and yield stress.
  • Concrete extrudability and material flow.
  • Layer-by-layer buildability.
  • Early-age structural behaviour.
  • Experimental failure investigation.
  • Numerical modelling and experimental validation.
Experimental + Numerical

Buildability Investigation

Buildability describes the ability of freshly printed concrete layers to support their own weight and the increasing load imposed by subsequent layers without excessive deformation or collapse.

Experimental investigation

  • Layer-by-layer printing of concrete elements.
  • Monitoring deformation as printed height increased.
  • Identification of shear-type and stability failure.
  • Determination of practical buildability limits.

Numerical investigation

Numerical modelling using the Finite Difference Method (FDM) was used to simulate the progressively increasing printed structure and predict stress development, deformation and failure.

Key objective

Predict when and how a printed element becomes structurally unstable as additional concrete layers are deposited.

Buildability: Experimental vs Numerical

Experimental buildability behaviour compared with numerical simulation using the Finite Difference Method (FDM).

Extrudability: Experimental vs DEM Simulation

Experimental concrete extrusion compared with numerical particle-flow simulation using the Discrete Element Method (DEM).

Material Flow

Extrudability Investigation

Extrudability represents the ability of the fresh concrete to move through the delivery system and nozzle while producing a continuous, dimensionally stable filament without blockage, tearing or excessive deformation.

Experimental investigation

  • Controlled extrusion through the printing nozzle.
  • Assessment of continuous material flow.
  • Evaluation of filament geometry and stability.
  • Observation of material flow and bulging behaviour.

Numerical investigation

The Discrete Element Method (DEM) was used to investigate particle-level material flow through the extrusion system and reproduce the behaviour observed experimentally.

Key objective

Understand how material behaviour, extrusion rate and nozzle conditions influence the consistency and geometry of the printed filament.

Integrated Research Approach

Combining experiments, material behaviour and numerical analysis to understand the complete concrete 3D printing process.

RMIT Research & Sustainable Construction

Sustainable Recycled Materials for Construction

Research focused on converting low-grade natural and waste materials into practical supplementary cementitious materials for lower-carbon concrete, supported by advanced laboratory and synchrotron characterisation.

From Low-Grade Resources to Low-Carbon Concrete

The research combines material activation, concrete performance testing, microstructural analysis and synchrotron X-ray techniques to understand how Australian natural and waste clays can be transformed into useful supplementary cementitious materials.

Selected media coverage highlighting the RMIT research on converting low-grade clay into a supplementary cementitious material for lower-carbon concrete.

Research Impact & Media

Common Low-Grade Clay Strengthens Low-Carbon Concrete

My RMIT research investigated the potential of abundant Australian low-grade and waste clays as supplementary cementitious materials for reducing the environmental impact of concrete.

The research demonstrated that appropriate activation of low-grade clay can improve its suitability for use in cementitious systems, creating an opportunity to utilise locally available materials that may otherwise have limited commercial value.

The findings attracted attention across construction and materials-industry media, helping communicate the broader potential of calcined-clay technology for lower-carbon and more resource-efficient construction.

International Research Facility

European Synchrotron Radiation Facility

The European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in Grenoble, France, provides high-brilliance X-ray beamlines that enable investigation of cementitious materials from atomic-scale structure through to three-dimensional microstructure.

As part of my RMIT research, synchrotron experiments were used to investigate calcined-clay activation, hydration behaviour, structural disorder and microstructural evolution in cementitious systems.

The experimental program complemented conventional laboratory testing with advanced X-ray characterisation unavailable through standard laboratory instruments.

Multi-Scale Synchrotron Characterisation

Different ESRF beamlines were used to investigate material structure from three-dimensional microstructure to crystalline and local atomic-scale behaviour.

ESRF ID19

X-ray Micro-CT

High-resolution synchrotron X-ray computed tomography was used to investigate internal three-dimensional microstructure, including clinker, hydration products, pores and material heterogeneity.

Scale: Microstructure / 3D volume

ESRF ID22

High-Resolution XRD & PDF Analysis

In-situ high-energy X-ray diffraction and Pair Distribution Function analysis were used to investigate crystalline and amorphous phase evolution during cement hydration, providing insight into mineral transformations and reaction processes.

Scale: Crystalline and amorphous phase evolution

ESRF ID31

Fast XRD & PDF Analysis

High-energy X-ray diffraction together with Pair Distribution Function (PDF) analysis was used to examine crystalline phases and local / medium-range atomic structure during clay calcination.

Scale: Crystalline + local atomic structure

Synchrotron technique clarification: ID19 was used for synchrotron X-ray micro-CT, ID22 for high-resolution XRD/PDF, and ID31 for fast XRD/PDF

UNSW Research & Sustainable Infrastructure

End-of-Life Tyre Based Concrete Noise Panels

Industry-linked research focused on developing sustainable precast concrete noise-barrier materials incorporating end-of-life tyre rubber, recycled glass and other recycled constituents for transport infrastructure applications.

Sustainable Precast Infrastructure

From Waste Tyres to Infrastructure Products

The project investigated how end-of-life tyre rubber and recycled constituents could be incorporated into concrete for precast traffic noise-wall applications while maintaining the engineering performance required for practical infrastructure use.

My role covered material development, concrete production, testing, data interpretation, industry engagement and assessment of engineering performance against transport-infrastructure requirements.

Key research areas

  • End-of-life tyre rubber concrete.
  • Recycled glass, manufactured sand and recycled aggregate utilisation.
  • Precast noise-barrier applications.
  • Mechanical, durability and acoustic performance.
  • Industry validation and prototype development.
Material Development

Mix Design, Testing & Validation

A systematic experimental program was used to develop recycled-material concrete mixes and evaluate their suitability for precast noise-wall manufacturing.

Experimental activities

  • Trial batching and optimisation of concrete mixes.
  • Fresh-state and workability assessment.
  • Preparation and controlled curing of specimens.
  • Compressive, flexural and other mechanical testing.
  • Durability and microstructural characterisation.
  • Acoustic-performance assessment for noise-barrier use.

Engineering Challenge

Increasing rubber content can reduce early-age concrete strength. The research therefore examined early strength development and mix behaviour to support safe stripping of precast elements and improve manufacturing efficiency.
